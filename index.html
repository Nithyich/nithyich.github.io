What I do

Delivery, end to end

Two halves of the same job: getting the integration technically right, and putting the governance around it that keeps it right once it's someone else's to run.

Governance & controls

Turning a delivery into something auditable: clear ownership, decision records, defined escalation paths, and controls proportionate to the actual risk rather than ceremony for its own sake. If nobody can say who decided what and when, the platform isn't governed — it's just running.

Commercial operations

The operational layer between what was sold and what gets delivered — scope and consumption against what was contracted, and reporting that lets commercial, delivery and risk teams argue from the same numbers instead of two spreadsheets.

Risk & assurance

Identifying where a delivery is actually exposed — data quality, regulatory fitness, key-person dependency, third-party reliance — and tracking it somewhere visible with an owner and a date, rather than in the memory of whoever noticed first.

Data mapping & schema translation

Taking a customer's field list — often ambiguous, often mislabelled — and resolving each one to the authoritative concept in the target data model, with an explicit confidence score and an honest no-match where the data genuinely isn't there.

API enablement & documentation

Reference collections that mirror the operational journey rather than the endpoint list, so a migrating team can map its existing process onto new concepts instead of reverse-engineering them from a spec.

Internal tooling

When the same manual work happens for the third time, I build the tool. React front-ends that encode the methodology, keep the schema as the source of truth, and export something a customer can actually act on.

01

Client Onboarding Workstreams & Delivery Governance

Moody's Analytics

  • Scaled agile
  • ERP integration
  • Governance
  • Risk

Coordinating enterprise client onboarding workstreams for credit analytics and risk platforms, including the integration workflows connecting client ERP systems to Moody's data APIs — and running the governance review cycle that surrounds those deliveries.

Problem

  • Onboarding delay rarely traces to the technology. It traces to a requirement everyone agreed with and nobody defined precisely enough to verify.
  • Client, product, engineering and data teams each describe the same delivery in a different vocabulary.
  • A review board is only as good as what reaches it. Half the deferrals I saw came down to a paper that had dodged the hardest question rather than answered it.

What I did

  • Developed functional specifications and test plans for new platform modules in a scaled agile delivery environment.
  • Managed integration dependencies across client ERP systems and Moody's data APIs, surfacing blockers while there was still time to sequence around them.
  • Drove requirement clarification to the point of being testable, and owned test readiness — environments, data and acceptance criteria confirmed before a cycle opens.
  • Run a weekly delivery-review cadence covering roughly 6–8 active client issues and implementation dependencies, with requirement packs, releases and change requests tracked as Jira tickets.
  • Hold dependencies and readiness across up to five concurrent enterprise onboarding workstreams, with product, engineering, operations and risk represented.
  • Chair the sessions: set which items are tabled, make sure the people who can actually decide are in the room, and hold the discussion to an outcome rather than a round of updates. Director-level escalation is reserved for material delivery, commercial or risk issues.
  • Work through submissions with teams beforehand. The strongest predictor of a clean approval is whether the paper meets its hardest objection head-on, so that is what I push them to write.
  • Own the written record — the argument, the decision, who picked up what, and by when — plus a forward schedule visible far enough ahead that teams prepare instead of scramble.
  • Prune the process itself. Standardising the readiness inputs cut review-pack preparation from around two hours to thirty minutes per item; a step that survives out of habit costs a team exactly as much as one that earns its place.

Outcome

  • Roughly 50–60% of items now clear first pass without deferral, against a baseline where a missing answer routinely sent a paper back a cycle.
  • Review-pack preparation down from about two hours to thirty minutes per item, with readiness inputs standardised rather than assembled from scratch each week.
  • Submission quality is coached upstream rather than corrected in the room — teams arrive knowing what they will be pressed on.
  • The written record carries enough authority that a settled question stays settled, which is most of what stops a board relitigating itself. Decisions and actions are tracked to closure in Jira.

Weekly cadence · 5 concurrent workstreams · review prep 2 hrs → 30 min

04 — Approach

How I work

Two methods for two halves of the job. Both come from the same instinct: write the rule down, apply it consistently, and make the gaps visible rather than quietly absorbing them.

Mapping a customer field to a data model

The methodology encoded in the EVA Data Mapper. It exists because the failure mode in entity data integration is never a missing endpoint — it's a field that was force-fitted to something that looked close enough.

  1. Start with client intent

    Determine what business fact the client actually wants, not just the field name. The label is often misleading — "Company Name" could mean the legal registered name, the trading name, or the international name.

  2. Locate the authoritative concept

    Find the canonical concept in the data dictionary — checking definition and business meaning, and whether it is firmographic, legal, an identifier, or relationship-based. A registration number is not a VAT number, an LEI, or a local tax ID.

  3. Validate it exists in the response

    Confirm the field truly appears in the actual JSON response, and establish the full path — including whether it is nested, inside an array, or wrapped with metadata. The dictionary and the payload do not always agree.

  4. Choose the most authoritative variant

    Rank the candidates by explicit authority rules: legal and registered over inferred, primary over alternative, headquarters over branch.

  5. Identify the mapping type

    Classify it — one-to-one, one-to-many where the value is assembled from several fields, complex where a transformation is required, or no-match.

  6. Assign a confidence score

    Score on semantic alignment, data-type compatibility, structural complexity, and regulatory fitness. High is an exact semantic and structural match; medium means it works but needs transformation.

  7. Document the full JSON path

    Record the complete path from root, including array indices, resolving to the value unless the metadata is genuinely needed. Paths are never abbreviated.

  8. Call out gaps explicitly

    If the data model doesn't support a client field, mark it a no-match and say so. Never force-fit. This is what protects data quality, regulatory correctness, and the credibility of the product.

Putting governance around a delivery

Governance earns its cost when it shortens the distance between a problem appearing and the right person hearing about it. Everything below is in service of that; anything that isn't gets cut.

  1. Name the decisions before the meetings

    Start from the decisions the delivery will actually have to make, and work back to who owns each one. A forum without a decision list becomes a status update, and status updates don't unblock anything.

  2. Define what "ready" means in writing

    Stage gates need entry and exit criteria set down before anyone is up against the date. Once the deadline is close, criteria written in the moment will always bend towards the answer people want.

  3. Make scope and entitlement explicit

    What was contracted, what is being delivered, and what is being consumed are three different numbers. Reconciling them early turns a renewal surprise into a routine conversation.

  4. Size risk against exposure, not anxiety

    Rank by plausible impact and likelihood, not by whoever raised it most recently. Every item gets an owner, a mitigation and a review date — an unowned risk is a note, not a control.

  5. Report the same numbers to everyone

    One source for status, risk and dependency, reaching commercial, delivery and risk in the same form. Divergent reporting is where disputes start, and reconciling versions costs more than producing one properly.

  6. Escalate on a threshold, not a mood

    Agree in advance what breaches the line — slip, spend, severity — so escalation is a rule being followed rather than someone choosing to spend political capital.

  7. Leave it runnable by someone else

    Document the cadence, the criteria and the handoffs so the governance survives the person who set it up. If it only works while you're in the room, it hasn't been built yet.
